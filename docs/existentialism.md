# Existentialism

Existentialism is a philosophical movement of the twentieth century centered on the problems of individual human existence, freedom, choice, and the search for meaning in a world without a pre-given order. Its roots reach back to the nineteenth century, to the work of Søren Kierkegaard and Friedrich Nietzsche, while its flourishing came in the mid-twentieth century in France and Germany.

## Existence Precedes Essence

The central thesis, formulated by Jean-Paul Sartre, states that existence precedes essence. This means that a human being first appears in the world and only afterward defines who they are through their actions. A person has no pre-given nature or purpose — unlike, for example, a knife, whose essence (to cut) is fixed before it is made. Human beings create themselves through their actions and bear full responsibility for who they become.

## Freedom and Responsibility

Existentialists emphasize the radical freedom of the human being. Sartre argued that a person is "condemned to be free": even the refusal to choose is itself a choice. This freedom gives rise to a heavy burden of responsibility, since in choosing for oneself a person affirms an image of what a human being ought to be in general. Connected with this is the concept of anguish (or dread) — the experience that arises in the face of one's own absolute freedom and the absence of external supports.

## The Absurd

Albert Camus developed the idea of the absurd — the contradiction between the human longing for meaning and a silent, indifferent world that offers none. In "The Myth of Sisyphus," Camus examines whether life is worth living if it lacks higher meaning. His answer is revolt: a person must accept the absurd and live in spite of it, resorting neither to suicide nor to the false comforts of religion. Sisyphus, condemned to roll his rock uphill forever, Camus invites us to imagine as happy.

## Authenticity and Bad Faith

An important concept of existentialism is authenticity. To live authentically means to accept one's freedom and responsibility without hiding behind imposed roles and the expectations of others. Sartre called the opposite of authenticity "bad faith" — a self-deception in which a person denies their own freedom, pretending that their actions are determined by circumstances, a role, or their nature.

## Key Figures

Existentialism includes Søren Kierkegaard, regarded as its forerunner, who wrote about faith and individual choice; Friedrich Nietzsche with his idea of the revaluation of values; Martin Heidegger, who analyzed being and time; Jean-Paul Sartre, the chief systematizer of the movement; Albert Camus, associated with the philosophy of the absurd; and Simone de Beauvoir, who applied existentialist ideas to questions of freedom and the condition of women.

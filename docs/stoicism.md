# Stoicism

Stoicism is a school of philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens. Its name comes from the "Stoa Poikile" (Painted Porch), the colonnade where Zeno taught his students. The school lasted for several centuries and had an enormous influence on Roman culture and later on European ethics.

## The Core Idea

At the heart of Stoicism lies the distinction between what is within our power and what is not. Within our power are our judgments, impulses, desires, and evaluations. Not within our power are the body, property, reputation, the actions of others, and external events. The source of suffering, according to the Stoics, is the attempt to control what cannot be controlled and emotional attachment to things that do not depend on us. The wise person focuses solely on what is subject to their own will and accepts everything else with equanimity.

## Living According to Nature

The Stoics held that the goal of human life is to live in agreement with nature, that is, with the reason that pervades the whole cosmos. The world is governed by a universal rational principle they called the logos. Virtue consists in bringing one's will into harmony with the rational order of the world. A person who accepts the course of events as an expression of the cosmic order attains inner freedom regardless of external circumstances.

## Virtue as the Only Good

For the Stoics, the only genuine good is virtue, and the only genuine evil is vice. Everything else — health, wealth, fame, pleasure — belongs to the category of "indifferents." This does not mean that health and sickness are equivalent in practice; the Stoics called health a "preferred indifferent." But none of these things makes a person truly good or happy. Happiness, or eudaimonia, is achieved only through moral excellence and the right attitude toward what happens.

## Key Figures

Three Roman Stoics are best known. Seneca was a statesman and tutor to Nero, author of moral letters. Epictetus was a former slave who became a teacher of philosophy, whose discourses were recorded by his student Arrian. Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor whose personal notes, the "Meditations," became one of the most widely read philosophical texts on self-discipline and the acceptance of fate.

## Practices

Stoicism was not only a theory but also a set of practical exercises. Among them were evening reflection on the day just lived, the premeditation of possible misfortunes (premeditatio malorum) so as not to be caught off guard, and the exercise of distinguishing impressions from one's judgments about them. These practices have made Stoicism popular again today, especially in the context of psychological resilience.

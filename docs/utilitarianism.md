# Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is an ethical theory according to which the moral worth of an action is determined by its consequences, namely by how much it increases overall well-being. The right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people. Utilitarianism belongs to the consequentialist theories, meaning it judges actions by their results rather than by intentions or conformity to rules.

## The Principle of Utility

At the foundation of utilitarianism lies the principle of utility (or the greatest happiness principle). Jeremy Bentham, the founder of classical utilitarianism, defined utility as whatever produces pleasure or prevents pain. Morality, for Bentham, reduces to an arithmetic of pleasures and pains: one must calculate how much good and how much harm each possible action would bring to everyone affected, and choose the one that yields the greatest net balance of pleasure over pain.

## The Hedonic Calculus

Bentham proposed a kind of "calculus of happiness" — a way of measuring pleasures and pains along several dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, and purity. In classical utilitarianism, pleasures differ only quantitatively: any pleasure is in principle comparable to any other. Bentham's famous remark holds that, given an equal quantity of pleasure, a simple children's game is as good as poetry.

## Higher and Lower Pleasures

John Stuart Mill, developing Bentham's ideas, introduced an important qualification: pleasures differ not only in quantity but also in quality. He divided pleasures into higher (intellectual, moral, aesthetic) and lower (bodily), and argued that the higher ones are more valuable. Mill wrote that it is better to be a dissatisfied human being than a satisfied pig, and better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. In this way he tried to defend utilitarianism against the charge of being a "philosophy fit for swine."

## Objections and Difficulties

Utilitarianism faces a number of well-known objections. One is the problem of justice: the theory can in principle permit sacrificing the interests of a minority for the good of the majority, if doing so increases the total sum of happiness. Another is the difficulty, or practical impossibility, of accurately predicting and calculating all the consequences of an action. A third concerns individual rights, which in pure utilitarianism have no independent value and may be overridden for the common good. In response, rule utilitarianism arose, which evaluates not individual acts but adherence to rules that in general lead to the greatest good.

## Key Figures

The founder of utilitarianism is Jeremy Bentham, who formulated the principle of utility and the hedonic calculus. John Stuart Mill developed and softened the doctrine, introducing the qualitative distinction of pleasures and linking utilitarianism to ideas of individual liberty. Henry Sidgwick gave the most systematic exposition of classical utilitarianism. In the twentieth century, the ideas of consequentialism were advanced by R. M. Hare and by Peter Singer, who applied utilitarianism to questions of bioethics and the treatment of animals.

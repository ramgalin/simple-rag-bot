# Empiricism

Empiricism is a position in the theory of knowledge holding that the source of all knowledge is sensory experience. According to empiricism, the mind at birth is like a "blank slate" (tabula rasa), and all the content of our thinking derives from sensations and observations. Empiricism stands opposed to rationalism, which regards reason and innate ideas as the primary source of knowledge.

## Experience as the Basis of Knowledge

Empiricists hold that there are no innate ideas: everything we know we obtain from perception of the external world or from observation of our own inner states. John Locke, one of the founders of the movement, argued that the mind of a newborn is empty and is filled with impressions as experience accumulates. Even the most abstract concepts, according to empiricists, are ultimately reducible to the simple sense data out of which they are composed.

## British Empiricism

The classic form of empiricism took shape in the work of three British thinkers of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. John Locke laid the foundations, distinguishing primary qualities (extension, shape, motion) inherent in things themselves from secondary qualities (color, taste, smell) arising in perception. George Berkeley went further, asserting that to exist is to be perceived (esse est percipi) and denying the existence of matter independent of perception. David Hume pushed empiricism to its skeptical conclusions.

## Hume's Skepticism

David Hume showed that many familiar notions have no firm basis in experience. In particular, he analyzed the concept of causation and concluded that we never observe the causal connection itself — we see only that one event regularly follows another, and out of habit we expect the pattern to repeat. The so-called necessary connection between cause and effect, according to Hume, is a projection of our psychological habit rather than a fact given in experience. This conclusion called into question the foundations of science and, in his own words, awoke Immanuel Kant from his "dogmatic slumber."

## Role in the Scientific Method

Empiricism is closely tied to the rise of experimental science. Francis Bacon, a forerunner of the movement, championed the inductive method: moving from particular observations to general laws. The idea that theories should be tested by observation and experiment, rather than deduced from pure speculation, became the foundation of modern natural science. Later these commitments were developed by logical positivism, which demanded that meaningful statements be verifiable by experience.

## Key Figures

The principal figures of empiricism are Francis Bacon with his method of induction, John Locke as the systematizer of the doctrine of experience, George Berkeley with his subjective idealism, and David Hume with his skeptical analysis of causation and induction. The empirical tradition also counts later thinkers such as John Stuart Mill and the logical positivists of the twentieth century.
